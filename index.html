Why Do Consumers Feel Rising Inflation?

We bring together four views of inflation, showing how broad price pressure becomes visible through everyday costs such as energy, housing, food, and transportation.

Overall CPI and Energy CPI

Energy Prices Made Inflation Feel More Volatile

From 2023 to 2026, overall consumer prices rose steadily, while energy prices followed a more volatile line. The overall CPI climbed from about 300 to over 330, reflecting persistent inflation across the economy.

In contrast, the Energy CPI fluctuated throughout most of the period, falling during parts of 2023 and 2024 before stabilizing in 2025. The highest change occurred in early 2026, when energy prices surged sharply, narrowing the gap with the overall CPI.

This highlights a recurring feature of inflation, while prices for most goods and services tend to increase gradually, energy costs are far more sensitive to shifts in supply, demand, and global market conditions.

According to the Associated Press, This sharp increase in energy prices during early 2026 occured with the escalation of the Iran conflict, which contributed to rising oil prices as markets reacted to the risk of tighter energy supplies.

Due to the shutdown of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical route for global energy shipments. There was anticipated potential shortages, crude oil prices rose significantly, increasing the cost of gasoline, transportation, and other energy-related goods.

These geopolitical tensions contributed to the surge in the Energy CPI, demonstrating how international events can quickly influence domestic energy prices and amplify inflationary pressures felt by consumers.

Shelter CPI

Housing: The Inflation That Won't Quit

Year-over-year percent change in the U.S. shelter CPI (CUSR0000SAH1), Bureau of Labor Statistics via FRED. The dashed line marks the pre-pandemic average. The shaded region covers 2024 to the present.

Pandemic demand met a severe supply shortage

Remote work and near-zero interest rates sent housing demand surging in 2021, while the U.S. was already roughly 3–4 million homes short of what demand required after years of underbuilding. Rents in many cities jumped 15–30% in a single year.

Source: Axios: The U.S. Affordable Housing Shortage

Rate hikes helped — but a built-in lag kept the index rising

About a third of the CPI is "Owners' Equivalent Rent," a survey measure that trails real-market rents by 12–18 months. So even as actual rents stabilized in late 2022, the official shelter index kept climbing into 2023 — its fastest pace since the 1980s.

Source: NBER: Understanding the Lag Between CPI Shelter Inflation and Market Rents

Growth is slowing in 2024–2026, but prices haven't fallen

The year-over-year rate has returned near its pre-pandemic average, yet the cumulative increase since January 2020 is ~32%. New tariffs on lumber and steel in 2025 are making new construction more expensive, keeping supply constrained just when it's needed most.

Source: MarketWatch: Shelter from Inflation? CPI Not Showing Much Sign of It

Transportation CPI, Jan. 2024 to Apr. 2026

Transportation Inflation Had One Clear Spike in Early 2026

This chart shows the monthly percent change in the Transportation CPI. Bars above zero mean transportation prices rose from the previous month. Bars below zero mean they fell.

Main reading

The clearest transportation inflation pressure point in this bar chart is March 2026, when Transportation CPI rose a shocking 4.28% from the previous month. This spike lines up with reporting on the Iran war’s effect on oil and gasoline prices. CBS News reported that the Iran war triggered a global energy shock, with gasoline prices rising 21.2% from February to March. AP News also reported that gas prices were pushing inflation higher and squeezing household budgets.

April 2026 continued the same pattern rather than becoming a separate story. Transportation CPI rose again after the March spike, which suggests that the pressure did not disappear immediately. The Guardian reported that April inflation rose at its fastest pace in three years because of higher energy prices connected to the Iran war. Yahoo Finance also explained that the March CPI breakdown showed the Iran war sending gas prices sharply higher while airfare kept climbing because of higher jet fuel costs. This supports the interpretation that consumers may have felt inflation through gasoline, commuting, delivery, and travel costs.

This connects the transportation CPI bar chart to the Trump administration because the March and April 2026 increases happened during a geopolitical fuel shock that became a public economic problem while the White House was publicly defending and downplaying the economic toll of the Iran war. The Dallas Fed projected that oil prices would peak in April and May 2026 and keep headline inflation elevated into the third quarter of 2026. The New York Times reported that inflation rose to 3.3% in March and 3.8% in April after the war began, while gas prices were about 53% higher than they were at the start of the war. This supports the claim that transportation inflation became part of the public cost of the war: consumers felt it through gasoline, commuting, shipping, and travel costs, even as Trump argued that the price pressure was temporary.
